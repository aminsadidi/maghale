\begin{abstract}
Near the tip of a gold nanorod the decay rate of a quantum emitter depends strongly on the orientation of its
transition dipole. Part of this anisotropy is generic---any metal surface quenches a perpendicular dipole more
strongly than a parallel one---and part is due to the longitudinal plasmon resonance of the elongated particle,
but the two have not been separated quantitatively. We do so for a capped gold nanorod (length \SI{60}{nm},
diameter \SI{20}{nm}) with converged boundary-element calculations that reproduce the exact Mie solution for the
equal-volume sphere to about \SI{1}{\percent}, and with a quasistatic plasmon-eigenmode decomposition of the decay
rate. At a \SI{5}{nm} gap the axial dipole reaches a Purcell factor of \num{1.64e3} and a radiative enhancement of
117, and \SI{91}{\percent} of the rate enhancement is carried by a single mode, the longitudinal dipolar plasmon.
The remaining, non-resonant part has an axial-to-transverse ratio of about three, close to that of the
equal-volume sphere (2.8--3.0); the resonance raises the ratio to 49 for the total and 329 for the radiative rate.
Because the quenching background decays faster with distance than the coupling to the mode, the anisotropy is
largest not at the smallest gap but at about \SI{10}{nm} (136), and it grows with the aspect ratio from 19 to 872
between aspect ratios 2 and 5, while the non-resonant factor stays near three for all shapes. Translating the results into design rules, we show that the optimal gap
grows with the intrinsic quantum yield of the emitter, quantify the tolerance to the emitter position, the effect
of a glass substrate and of nonlocal response, and give resonance-matched antenna parameters for representative
emitters. Three-dimensional and body-of-revolution finite-difference time-domain calculations agree with the
boundary-element results for the radiative quantities but underestimate (by \SI{9}{\percent}) or fail to converge
for the total rate in the nanometre gap.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Purcell effect; local density of optical states; gold nanorod; plasmon eigenmodes;
quenching; single-photon source; boundary element method.

\section{Introduction}

The spontaneous emission rate of a quantum emitter is not an intrinsic property but depends on its
electromagnetic environment~\cite{purcell,novotny}. Metallic nanoparticles supporting localized surface plasmon
resonances confine light to volumes far below the diffraction limit and can enhance emission rates by several
orders of magnitude~\cite{maier,vanhulst,bharadwaj2009}. This has made plasmonic nanoantennas a candidate
platform for bright, fast single-photon sources~\cite{koenderink,hoang,akselrod,senellart}, in which a large
rate enhancement shortens the excited-state lifetime and raises the achievable repetition rate, and a short
radiative lifetime relative to the dephasing time is a prerequisite for indistinguishable photons~\cite{aharonovich}.

The price of plasmonic confinement is absorption. At emitter--metal separations of a few nanometres, energy is
transferred to non-radiative channels in the metal, and the fluorescence is quenched rather than
enhanced~\cite{anger,kuhn,ford,carminati2006}. The total decay rate and the radiative rate therefore have to be
treated separately, and their ratio---the antenna quantum efficiency---depends sensitively on distance,
wavelength and the intrinsic quantum yield of the emitter~\cite{bharadwaj2007,khurgin,lu2020}.

Gold nanorods are attractive antennas because their longitudinal plasmon resonance is tunable through the
aspect ratio across the visible and near infrared~\cite{ni2008,sonnichsen}, they can be synthesised with good
control~\cite{nikoobakht}, and their tips concentrate the near field. Single-molecule experiments have
demonstrated fluorescence enhancements exceeding a thousandfold near individual gold nanorods~\cite{yuan,khatua},
down to emitters with quantum yields of $10^{-4}$~\cite{lu2020}. On the theory side, Mohammadi \emph{et al.}
computed radiative enhancements and antenna efficiencies for gold nanorods and nanospheroids as a function of
aspect ratio, volume and embedding medium~\cite{mohammadi,mohammadi2009}, and body-of-revolution solvers have
recently been extended to excitation and emission near core--shell rods~\cite{ugwuoke2026}. These studies, which
exploit the rotational symmetry of an emitter on the rod axis, consider the axial dipole and gaps of
\SI{10}{nm} or more.

A consequence of the nanorod's elongated shape is a strong dependence of the response on the orientation of the
emitter's transition dipole. Yet a dipole close to any metal surface---a sphere or a plane---also shows an
orientation dependence: in the quasi-static near field, energy transfer to the metal is twice as strong for a
dipole perpendicular to the surface as for a parallel one~\cite{ford}. It is therefore not obvious how much of the
anisotropy near a nanorod tip is generic and how much is caused by the longitudinal resonance that distinguishes
the nanorod from a compact particle. The distinction matters in practice: emitters such as colloidal quantum
dots, nanodiamonds or dye molecules are often randomly oriented, and orientation-averaged or scalar
descriptions---a single ``Purcell factor''---discard precisely the resonant part. A second, methodological issue
is that the total rate in a nanometre gap is difficult to converge with volume-discretisation methods, whereas the
radiative rate is not; conclusions about quenching then depend on the solver.

In this work we separate the two contributions in two independent ways. First, we compare the nanorod with an
equal-volume gold sphere, probed with axial (radial) and transverse (tangential) dipoles at the same gap. Second,
we expand the decay rate in the quasistatic plasmon eigenmodes of the particle~\cite{mayergoyz2005} and isolate
the contribution of the longitudinal dipolar mode from the non-resonant background. All rates are computed with a
boundary-element method validated against the exact Mie solution, and checked against two finite-difference
time-domain solvers. We then translate the results into design rules for plasmon-enhanced single-photon sources:
the dependence of the optimal gap on the emitter's intrinsic quantum yield, the tolerance to its position, the
effect of a substrate and of nonlocal response, and resonance-matched antennas for representative emitters.

\section{Theory}

\subsection{Decay rates and efficiency}
For a point dipole $\mathbf{p}=p\,\hat{\mathbf n}$ at $\mathbf r_0$ in the weak-coupling regime, the total decay
rate normalised to its value in the homogeneous host is~\cite{novotny}
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\Gamma_{\rm tot}}{\Gamma_0} = \frac{6\pi c}{\omega}\,
  \hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\mathrm{Im}\,\overleftrightarrow{\mathbf G}(\mathbf r_0,\mathbf r_0;\omega)\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}
  \equiv \Fp(\omega),
  \label{eq:ldos}
\end{equation}
where $\overleftrightarrow{\mathbf G}$ is the dyadic Green function. Classically, $\Fp=P_{\rm tot}/P_0$ is the
power delivered by a constant-amplitude dipole relative to the homogeneous host. The power radiated to the far
field, $T=P_{\rm rad}/P_0$, gives the radiative enhancement, and $\Fp-T$ is absorbed in the metal. For an emitter
of intrinsic quantum yield $q_0$, the quantum efficiency in the presence of the antenna is~\cite{bharadwaj2007}
\begin{equation}
  \eta(q_0) = \frac{T}{\Fp + (1-q_0)/q_0},
  \label{eq:eta}
\end{equation}
which reduces to the antenna efficiency $\eta_a=T/\Fp$ for $q_0=1$. The total decay rate of the emitter is
multiplied by $q_0\Fp+1-q_0$.

For a dipole on the symmetry axis of a body of revolution the Green tensor is diagonal, with an axial component
and two equal transverse ones; the anisotropy is fully characterised by the ratios
$\Fp^{\parallel}/\Fp^{\perp}$ and $T^{\parallel}/T^{\perp}$. For a sphere, axial and transverse correspond to radial
and tangential dipoles, for which exact Mie expressions exist~\cite{ruppin,kim,mertens}. The equal-volume sphere
therefore provides a reference that has the same amount of gold and the same dipole--surface gap, but no
elongated resonance.

\subsection{Plasmon-eigenmode decomposition}
In the quasistatic limit the surface charge $\sigma$ induced on a particle obeys the boundary integral equation
$(\Lambda + F)\,\sigma = -\phi'_{\rm ext}$, where $F$ is the normal derivative of the Coulomb kernel on the surface,
$\phi'_{\rm ext}$ the normal derivative of the external (dipole) potential, and
$\Lambda(\omega)=2\pi\,(\varepsilon+\varepsilon_b)/(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_b)$ contains the permittivities of
the metal and the host~\cite{mayergoyz2005,hohenester2012}. Expanding in the right and left eigenvectors of $F$,
$F u_k=\lambda_k u_k$, $\tilde u_k F=\lambda_k\tilde u_k$, $\tilde u_k u_l=\delta_{kl}$, gives
\begin{equation}
  \sigma = -\sum_k \frac{u_k\,(\tilde u_k\cdot\phi'_{\rm ext})}{\Lambda(\omega)+\lambda_k},
  \qquad
  \Fp-1 = \sum_k \Delta\Fp^{(k)},
  \label{eq:modes}
\end{equation}
because the induced field at the dipole, and hence $\Fp-1$, is linear in $\sigma$. Each mode resonates where
$\mathrm{Re}\,\Lambda(\omega)=-\lambda_k$, and its weight is the product of its coupling to the dipole
($\tilde u_k\cdot\phi'_{\rm ext}$) and of the field it produces back at the dipole. For the rod, the longitudinal
dipolar mode is identified as the eigenvector with the largest axial oscillator strength. On the axis, it couples
only to the axial dipole, whereas the transverse dipole couples only to modes with azimuthal number $m=\pm1$. We
call $\Delta\Fp^{(L)}$ the resonant part and $\Fp-1-\Delta\Fp^{(L)}$ the background; the background contains the
high-order modes that accumulate near the surface-plasmon condition and describe the lossy-surface (quenching)
channel~\cite{ford,anger}.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Geometry and material}
The nanorod is a gold cylinder of length \SI{40}{nm} and radius $R=\SI{10}{nm}$ terminated by two hemispherical
caps (total length $L=\SI{60}{nm}$, aspect ratio 3), a geometry accessible by seed-mediated
growth~\cite{nikoobakht}. The dipole is placed on the rod axis at a distance $d$ from the apex
($d=\SI{5}{nm}$ unless stated otherwise; \numrange{3}{30}{nm} in the gap study). The equal-volume sphere has
$R_{\rm sph}=\SI{15.874}{nm}$. The host is air unless stated. Gold is described by the tabulated
Johnson--Christy permittivity~\cite{johnson} in all boundary-element and Mie calculations.

\subsection{Boundary-element method}
The rates are computed with the boundary-element method (BEM) of the MNPBEM17
toolbox~\cite{hohenester2012,waxenegger2015,hohenester2018}, which solves the full Maxwell equations for the
surface charges and currents and discretises only the particle boundary, with curved boundary elements. $\Fp$
follows from the induced field at the dipole and $T$ from the far field, both in units of the rate in the
homogeneous host. The rod surface is discretised with element sizes $h=3$, 2 and \SI{1.5}{nm} (720, 1550 and 2829
elements), the sphere with 400, 784 and 1444 vertices; all gaps of one geometry are solved simultaneously. The
eigenmode decomposition, Eq.~\eqref{eq:modes}, uses the 40 lowest quasistatic eigenmodes of the
\SI{2}{nm} mesh; the full quasistatic $\Fp$ is computed with the same mesh. Additional calculations, described
where they are used, include a glass substrate (layer Green function~\cite{waxenegger2015}), the hydrodynamic
nonlocal response through the equivalent local cover layer of Luo \emph{et al.}~\cite{luo2013}, plane-wave
excitation, other shapes and silver. All calculations ran on GitHub-hosted runners; the scripts are provided.

\subsection{Finite-difference time-domain cross-checks}
The rod and sphere were also computed with a commercial three-dimensional FDTD solver (Lumerical FDTD 2024~R1,
\SI{2}{nm} override mesh)~\cite{taflove,lumerical} and with a body-of-revolution FDTD in cylindrical coordinates
(Meep~\cite{meep}, grids of 2, 1.33 and \SI{1}{nm}), in which an axial dipole couples only to the $m=0$ field
harmonic. Details, the gold Drude--Lorentz fit required by the time-domain solvers and all benchmarks are given in
the Supplementary Information.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Validation and convergence}
For the equal-volume sphere at a \SI{5}{nm} gap the BEM reproduces the exact Mie solution for both dipole
orientations over \SIrange{500}{900}{nm} (Fig.~\ref{fig:mie}): with 1444 vertices the median deviation is
\SI{0.9}{\percent} for the radial $\Fp$ and \SI{1.4}{\percent} for the radial $T$, \SI{0.7}{\percent} and
\SI{1.0}{\percent} for the tangential dipole, and at most \SI{4}{\percent}; the deviation is of the same size at 10 and
\SI{20}{nm} and in water (Table~S1). For the rod (axial dipole, \SI{5}{nm} gap) the peak values change by
\SI{0.3}{\percent} ($\Fp$) and \SI{0.4}{\percent} ($T$) between the 2- and \SI{1.5}{nm} meshes, and the peak positions
not at all; a \SI{1}{nm} mesh (6324 elements, \SIrange{500}{808}{nm}) changes them by a further \SI{0.2}{\percent}
(Table~S2, Fig.~S1). We quote the \SI{1.5}{nm} mesh. The three-dimensional FDTD reproduces the radiative peak
(119 at \SI{616}{nm}, BEM 117 at \SI{608}{nm}) but gives a \SI{9}{\percent} lower $\Fp$ (1496 versus 1638); the
body-of-revolution FDTD reproduces $T$ to \SI{7}{\percent} on a \SI{1}{nm} grid but does not converge for $\Fp$ in
the \SI{5}{nm} gap, where the staircased surface overestimates it by about a factor of two even for the sphere
(Supplementary Information). Volume discretisation is therefore adequate for the radiated power and the emission
pattern, but the total rate---and hence the quenching---in a nanometre gap requires a method that represents the
metal surface exactly.

\subsection{Axial versus transverse dipole, rod versus sphere}
Figure~\ref{fig:spectra} shows $\Fp$, $T$ and $\eta_a$ at a \SI{5}{nm} gap. For the axial dipole at the rod tip
$\Fp$ peaks at \num{1638} at \SI{604}{nm} and $T$ at 117 at \SI{608}{nm}, where $\eta_a=\SI{7.3}{\percent}$: more
than \SI{92}{\percent} of the energy is absorbed. A second $\Fp$ maximum at \SI{512}{nm} (848) coincides with the
onset of interband absorption and is almost purely non-radiative ($T=2.0$). The transverse dipole at the same
position has $T<0.5$ at all wavelengths---it radiates less than in free space---and its $\Fp$ maximum (248 at
\SI{508}{nm}) is non-radiative.

The equal-volume sphere separates the generic from the resonant contribution (Fig.~\ref{fig:orientation}). Its
radial-to-tangential ratio of $\Fp$ lies between 2.75 and 2.97 over the whole band, in agreement with the exact
solution (2.76--2.97) and near the quasi-static flat-surface value of two, without resonant structure. The rod
shows a similar ratio (3.3--4.0) below \SI{530}{nm} but rises to 49 at the longitudinal resonance. For the
radiated power the sphere ratio is 11--53, varying slowly with wavelength, because a tangential dipole near a metal
surface radiates poorly; the rod ratio is 5--6 below \SI{530}{nm} and 329 at resonance. At \SI{608}{nm} the rod
exceeds the sphere by factors of 15 in $\Fp$ and 23 in $T$, whereas the maxima of the two particles differ by only
2.0 in $\Fp$: with the same amount of gold, the elongated particle does not provide a larger total rate but moves a
radiative resonance out of the interband region.

\subsection{Mode decomposition}
The eigenmode analysis makes this separation explicit (Fig.~\ref{fig:modes}). The lowest non-trivial mode of the
rod is the longitudinal dipole ($\lambda_k=-5.01$, quasistatic resonance at \SI{595}{nm}, \SI{13}{nm} to the blue of
the retarded result); the transverse dipolar modes lie at $\lambda_k\approx-1.71$, which requires $\varepsilon\approx-1.75$ and therefore falls into the strongly damped interband region below \SI{500}{nm}. At a
\SI{5}{nm} gap and the resonance, \SI{91}{\percent} of $\Fp-1$ is carried by the longitudinal mode alone. The
remaining background is a smooth function of wavelength and its axial value exceeds the transverse rate by a
factor of 3.1, close to the sphere's ratio: \emph{the geometric anisotropy of the rod is that of any curved metal
surface, and the excess is the contribution of a single resonant mode.}

The two contributions depend differently on the gap (Fig.~\ref{fig:modes}b,c). The background decays as the
near-field of a dipole above a lossy surface, approximately as $d^{-3}$, whereas the coupling to the dipolar mode
decays on the length scale of the tip radius. The share of the dipolar mode therefore increases from
\SI{80}{\percent} at \SI{3}{nm} to \SI{96}{\percent} at \SI{10}{nm} and \SI{98}{\percent} at \SI{20}{nm}, and the
total anisotropy $\Fp^{\parallel}/\Fp^{\perp}$ is non-monotonic: in the full retarded calculation it is 19 at
\SI{3}{nm}, 49 at \SI{5}{nm}, 136 at \SI{10}{nm}, 128 at \SI{15}{nm} and 75 at \SI{20}{nm}. At small gaps the
quenching background, which both orientations share, dilutes the resonant contribution; at large gaps the
transverse rate approaches its free-space value while the coupling to the mode keeps decreasing. The same
decomposition for aspect ratios 2--5 and for the sphere (Fig.~\ref{fig:modes}b, Table~S3) shows that the
background ratio is a property of the curved metal surface rather than of the shape: it is 2.5--3.7 for all four
rods and 2.3--2.4 for the sphere at gaps of 3--\SI{10}{nm}. What changes with the shape is the weight of the
dipolar mode. For the sphere its resonance lies in the interband region and it carries only \SI{9}{\percent} of
$\Fp-1$ at \SI{5}{nm}; for the rods its share at \SI{5}{nm} rises from \SI{57}{\percent} (aspect ratio 2) to
\SI{91}{\percent}, \SI{98}{\percent} and \SI{99}{\percent} (aspect ratios 3, 4, 5), as the resonance moves away from
the interband edge and gains oscillator strength. In the retarded calculation the maximum anisotropy, always found
near \SI{10}{nm}, accordingly grows from 19 (aspect ratio 2) to 54, 136, 621 and 872 (aspect ratios 2.5, 3, 4,
5).

\subsection{Gap dependence and the quantum-yield design rule}
Increasing the gap from 3 to \SI{20}{nm} (Fig.~\ref{fig:gap}) leaves the radiative peak at \SIrange{608}{612}{nm}
while $\Fp$ falls from 3782 (at the interband maximum, \SI{508}{nm}) to 78 and $T$ from 214 to 8.2. The antenna
efficiency at the radiative peak increases only from \SI{6.6}{\percent} to \SI{11.6}{\percent}, whereas its
spectral maximum, reached far to the red of the resonance, rises from \SI{12}{\percent} to \SI{60}{\percent}. The
exact solution for the sphere shows the same trade-off (Fig.~\ref{fig:gap}, lower row).

For a single-photon source the relevant figure of merit depends on the operating regime. Under saturated
(continuous-wave or high-repetition) excitation the photon rate scales with the radiative rate, i.e.\ with $T$,
for any $q_0$. Under pulsed excitation with a period much longer than the excited-state lifetime the number of
photons per pulse scales with $\eta(q_0)$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:eta}~\cite{koenderink,hoang}. Figure~\ref{fig:q0} and
Table~\ref{tab:q0} show that the optimal gap depends on $q_0$. For $q_0=1$ the \SI{20}{nm} gap is best
(\SI{11.6}{\percent}) and the \SI{3}{nm} gap worst (\SI{6.6}{\percent}). For $q_0=0.01$ the order is reversed
beyond \SI{7}{nm}: gaps of 7 and \SI{10}{nm} raise the efficiency from \SI{1}{\percent} to \SI{7.4}{\percent} and
\SI{7.1}{\percent}, whereas \SI{20}{nm} gives \SI{4.8}{\percent}. A large $\Fp$ is needed to outcompete the
intrinsic non-radiative rate $(1-q_0)/q_0$, but beyond that point additional $\Fp$ only feeds absorption, so the
optimal gap increases with $q_0$.

\subsection{Excitation}
Fluorescence below saturation is also proportional to the excitation rate, i.e.\ to the local intensity
$|\mathbf E\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}|^2/|\mathbf E_0|^2$ at the emitter. For plane-wave illumination polarised along the
rod axis the axial intensity enhancement at a \SI{5}{nm} gap peaks at 119 at \SI{610}{nm}
(Fig.~S2), equal within \SI{2}{\percent} to the radiative enhancement $T=117$ at \SI{608}{nm}, as expected from
reciprocity for a dipolar antenna~\cite{novotny,bharadwaj2009}. At common pump wavelengths it is small: 3.2 at
\SI{405}{nm}, 3.0 at \SI{450}{nm} and 1.8 at \SI{532}{nm}, compared with 3.9, 3.9 and 6.7 for the sphere, whose
resonance lies near \SI{530}{nm}. Unless the emitter is pumped near the longitudinal resonance---for example through
an upper level of a molecule or quasi-resonantly for a quantum dot---the rod accelerates and redirects emission but
contributes little to excitation; for emitters pumped far above the gap the excitation enhancement is set by the
interband response and is no larger than for a compact particle.

\subsection{Robustness: emitter position, substrate and nonlocal response}
\emph{Emitter position.} Emitters are often attached to a dielectric spacer shell of fixed thickness rather than
placed exactly on the axis. We therefore moved the dipole over a shell of thickness $s$ around the hemispherical cap,
from the axis ($\theta=0$) to the start of the cylindrical side ($\theta=\SI{90}{\degree}$, a lateral offset of
$R+s$), with the dipole normal ($n$) or tangential ($t$) to the surface (Fig.~\ref{fig:offset}). For $s=\SI{5}{nm}$
the radiative enhancement of the normal dipole decreases only gradually, to \SI{68}{\percent} of its on-axis value at
$\theta=\SI{90}{\degree}$ (\SI{70}{\percent} for $s=\SI{10}{nm}$), and its efficiency from \SI{7.6}{\percent} to
\SI{6.8}{\percent}, because the longitudinal mode carries charge over the whole cap. Tangential dipoles remain dark
everywhere ($T\leq1.2$). For a spacer-shell geometry the relevant tolerance is therefore the orientation of the
transition dipole relative to the local surface normal, not the position on the cap.

\emph{Substrate.} In experiments the rod usually lies on a glass cover slip. We placed the rod with its axis
parallel to a glass half-space ($n=1.52$) at a height of \SI{1}{nm} (a ligand layer) and the dipole beyond the tip,
on the rod axis and parallel to the interface. The longitudinal resonance shifts from 608 to \SI{635}{nm}; at a
\SI{5}{nm} gap the radiated power rises to 266 and $\Fp$ to 2198 (both in units of the vacuum rate), and the antenna
efficiency increases from \SI{7.3}{\percent} to \SI{12.1}{\percent} (\SI{14.3}{\percent} and \SI{17.6}{\percent} at
10 and \SI{20}{nm}). Relative to the same dipole on bare glass, whose rate is 1.27 times the vacuum rate, the
enhancements are 209 ($T$) and \num{1.7e3} ($\Fp$). The substrate therefore does not weaken the conclusions; it
improves the efficiency, because the resonance moves further from the interband edge, and a dipole parallel to and close to a
glass interface radiates predominantly into the glass~\cite{novotny}, i.e.\ towards an oil-immersion objective (we
did not resolve the angular distribution with the substrate). The dipoles perpendicular to
the rod axis remain dark ($T<2.3$).

\emph{Nonlocal response.} The hydrodynamic correction, estimated with the equivalent cover layer of Luo
\emph{et al.}~\cite{luo2013,raza2015,mortensen}, blue-shifts the radiative peak by \SI{5}{nm} and lowers $\Fp$ and $T$
at resonance by \SIrange{12}{17}{\percent} at gaps of 5 and \SI{10}{nm}; the antenna efficiency changes by less than
0.4 percentage points and the anisotropy increases slightly (from 49 to 70 at \SI{5}{nm}), because the transverse,
surface-dominated channel is suppressed more strongly. At \SI{3}{nm} the non-radiative interband maximum of $\Fp$ is
reduced by \SI{27}{\percent}. These changes are of the order expected for the cover-layer model with the tabulated
gold permittivity and do not affect the conclusions, but they indicate that absolute values of $\Fp$ at gaps of
\SI{3}{nm} and below carry an additional model uncertainty of this size.

\subsection{Generality: aspect ratio, environment, size, shape and metal}
Figure~\ref{fig:aspect} shows the radiative enhancement and the antenna efficiency at the radiative peak for aspect
ratios 2--5 ($D=\SI{20}{nm}$) and for the aspect-ratio-3 rod in water. As the resonance moves from \SI{560}{nm}
to \SI{760}{nm}, $T$ at a \SI{5}{nm} gap rises from 18 to \num{1.0e3} and $\eta_a$ from \SI{2.5}{\percent} to
\SI{22.5}{\percent}, because the resonance leaves the interband region; in water the same rod resonates at
\SI{715}{nm} with $T=457$ and $\eta_a=\SI{19.8}{\percent}$. These trends agree with those found by Mohammadi
\emph{et al.} at a \SI{10}{nm} distance~\cite{mohammadi}; the efficiency at the radiative peak depends mainly on the
resonance wavelength and only weakly on the gap between 5 and \SI{20}{nm}. At fixed aspect ratio 3, the rod volume
matters: for diameters of 15, 20 and \SI{30}{nm} the efficiency at the radiative peak (\SI{5}{nm} gap) is
\SI{3.3}{\percent}, \SI{7.3}{\percent} and \SI{23.7}{\percent}, reflecting the growth of radiative damping with
volume, while the resonance shifts only from 605 to \SI{625}{nm} (\SI{14.0}{\percent} at \SI{615}{nm} for
$D=\SI{25}{nm}$). A prolate spheroid with the same length and diameter resonates at \SI{600}{nm} with $T=106$ and
$\eta_a=\SI{5.3}{\percent}$; its sharper apex (radius of curvature \SI{3.3}{nm}) raises the off-resonance ratio to
7.7, which shows that the geometric factor is set by the curvature of the surface on the scale of the gap. A silver rod of the same
dimensions resonates at \SI{505}{nm} with $T=\num{1.1e3}$ and $\eta_a=\SI{38}{\percent}$, and its anisotropy reaches
677 in $\Fp$. For all gold rods the non-resonant ratio, read at \SI{500}{nm}, is 2.9--3.8 at a \SI{5}{nm} gap, so the
separation into a geometric factor of about three and a resonant factor that grows with the oscillator strength
of the longitudinal mode holds generally.

\subsection{Collection efficiency}
The near-to-far-field transformation of the body-of-revolution FDTD runs gives the fraction of the radiated power
emitted into a cone around the rod axis; unlike $\Fp$, this quantity is grid-independent. At the radiative peak it
is \SI{1.2}{\percent} for $\mathrm{NA}=0.5$ and \SI{18.0}{\percent} for $\mathrm{NA}=0.9$, within \SI{7}{\percent}
of a bare axial dipole (\SI{1.3}{\percent} and \SI{19.4}{\percent}): the antenna multiplies the radiated power by two
orders of magnitude but does not redirect it, in contrast to multi-element antennas that are designed for
directivity~\cite{taminiau2008}. A rod standing along the optical axis of the collection objective therefore
wastes most of its enhancement, whereas a rod lying in the focal plane presents a dipole perpendicular to the
optical axis, for which the dipolar pattern gives about \SI{33}{\percent} at $\mathrm{NA}=0.9$.

\subsection{Design table for representative emitters}
Table~\ref{tab:emit} combines the aspect-ratio sweep with literature values of the emission wavelength and the
intrinsic quantum yield of four emitter classes: defects in hexagonal boron nitride ($q_0=0.87$ at
\SI{580}{nm}~\cite{nikolay2019}), the dye crystal violet used in single-molecule nanorod experiments
($q_0=0.02$~\cite{khatua}), NV centres in nanodiamonds, whose $q_0$ ranges from below 0.2 in \SI{25}{nm} crystals to
0.1--0.9 in \SI{100}{nm} crystals~\cite{mohtashami2013}, and CsPbI$_3$ nanocrystals ($q_0\approx0.9$~\cite{protesescu2015}).
For each emitter the rod length is chosen to maximise $T$ at the emission wavelength. Three conclusions follow. First,
the rate speed-up $q_0\Fp+1-q_0$ ranges from 10 to \num{2e3} and is largest for bright emitters, for which it is
also accompanied by a loss of efficiency ($\eta/q_0=0.05$--0.33): for a near-unity emitter a gold nanorod is a
device for speed and saturated brightness (through $T$), not for efficiency. Second, for poor emitters such as
crystal violet the antenna improves the efficiency itself, by a factor of 5.6 at gaps of 5--\SI{10}{nm}. Third, the
emission wavelength matters more than the gap: an emitter at \SI{690}{nm} coupled to a rod resonating at
\SI{675}{nm} reaches $\eta_a\approx\SI{20}{\percent}$ and $T>400$, five times the radiative enhancement available at
\SI{580}{nm}, where interband absorption limits both.

\section{Discussion}
The central result is that the orientation dependence of emission near a nanorod tip consists of two factors with
different physical origins. A non-resonant factor of about three, which is the same for the equal-volume sphere and
for rods of aspect ratio 2--5, describes how strongly the curved metal surface quenches a perpendicular relative to
a parallel dipole; it does not depend on the plasmon resonance. On top of it, a single mode---the longitudinal
dipolar plasmon---contributes a resonant factor that grows with the oscillator strength of that mode, from 19 to
872 between aspect ratios 2 and 5. The eigenmode decomposition and the equal-volume control lead independently to the
same separation, and the decomposition explains why the anisotropy peaks at an intermediate gap: the background is
shared by both orientations and dominates at small gaps, while the coupling to the mode decays more slowly.

For randomly oriented emitters on the axis, the averages are $\langle\Fp\rangle=(\Fp^{\parallel}+2\Fp^{\perp})/3$ and
likewise for $T$. At \SI{608}{nm} and a \SI{5}{nm} gap this gives $\langle\Fp\rangle=555$ and $\langle T\rangle=39$,
three times below the axial values, while $\langle T\rangle/\langle\Fp\rangle=\SI{7.0}{\percent}$ is almost the axial
efficiency because the axial channel dominates both averages. The ensemble is bimodal: one third of the orientations
is bright, while the transverse two thirds are quenched ($\eta_a=\SI{1.1}{\percent}$) and radiate less than in free
space ($T=0.35$). An enhancement factor measured on an orientation ensemble~\cite{yuan,khatua} therefore hides a
population of nearly dark emitters, which matters for single-emitter applications where each emitter's orientation
is fixed, and it suggests orientation-selective assembly or emitters with a well-defined transition dipole.

For single-photon sources the results translate into simple rules. The gap should be chosen according to the
emitter: 5--\SI{10}{nm} for poor emitters, for which the antenna raises the efficiency, and \SI{15}{nm} or more for
near-unity emitters, for which the antenna mainly accelerates the emission and raises the saturated photon rate at
the cost of efficiency. The emission wavelength should lie well to the red of the interband edge, above about
\SI{650}{nm} for gold, which also favours longer rods, larger diameters and a glass substrate. The excitation should
be resonant with the longitudinal mode if an excitation enhancement is wanted. Finally, because the antenna leaves
the dipolar emission pattern intact, the rod should lie in the focal plane of the collection optics.

Methodologically, our comparison shows that volume-discretisation solvers reproduce the radiated power, the
resonance and the emission pattern of a nanorod antenna but not the total rate in a \SI{5}{nm} gap. A
three-dimensional FDTD calculation with a conformal mesh underestimated it by \SI{9}{\percent}; a staircased
body-of-revolution FDTD overestimated it by a factor of two even on a \SI{1}{nm} grid. Statements about quenching,
efficiency and the optimal gap therefore require a surface method or careful convergence studies.

The limitations of the present study are the local-response description of gold beyond the hydrodynamic estimate,
which is adequate at gaps of a few nanometres but not in sub-nanometre gaps where tunnelling matters~\cite{xu,ciraci};
surface scattering, which adds to the damping of small particles~\cite{kreibig,coronado}; the classical treatment of
the emitter in the weak-coupling regime; and the quasistatic approximation of the eigenmode analysis, which shifts the
longitudinal resonance by \SI{13}{nm} and, lacking radiative coupling, gives anisotropy ratios about \SI{35}{\percent}
lower than the retarded ones (33, 82 and 47 versus 49, 136 and 75 at 5, 10 and \SI{20}{nm}) while reproducing their
gap dependence and the position of the maximum. A
quasinormal-mode treatment~\cite{sauvan,lalanne} would extend the decomposition to the retarded problem.

\section{Conclusion}
Using converged boundary-element calculations, an equal-volume sphere as a control and a plasmon-eigenmode
decomposition, we have separated the orientational anisotropy of the Purcell enhancement near a gold nanorod tip
into a geometric factor of about three, common to all curved metal surfaces, and a resonant factor carried by the
longitudinal dipolar mode, which reaches 49 in the total and 329 in the radiative rate for an aspect-ratio-3 rod at a
\SI{5}{nm} gap, peaks near a \SI{10}{nm} gap and grows with the aspect ratio. More than \SI{92}{\percent} of the
emitted power is absorbed at the Purcell maximum for a \SI{5}{nm} gap; the optimal gap for a single-photon source
depends on the intrinsic quantum yield of the emitter, and resonance-matched rods emitting above \SI{650}{nm}, on
glass and in the focal plane of the collection optics, offer the best compromise between speed, brightness and
efficiency. Simulation scripts, raw spectra and analysis code are openly available.
