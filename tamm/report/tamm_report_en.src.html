Numerical study based on the published data set

Origin of the ~90 nm red shift of the Ag / nanoparticle-DBR Tamm sensor: full 3D electrodynamic modelling

A. Hossain Sadidi, S. M. P. Molaei Tabari — Department of Physics, University of Isfahan (supervisor: Dr. M. Bagheri Harouni)
Data: Rossini et al., doi:10.1002/nap2.70138; data set doi:10.5281/zenodo.18064645  ·  November 2026

Summary

We asked which physical change of the device can produce the response reported for PBS and for VLP exposure: a red shift of the Tamm reflectance minimum by about 90 nm (PBS: +92.8 nm, mean of 5 tests; VLP 10 ng/mL: +88.5 nm), with a small change of the minimum reflectance for PBS (+2.2 %) and a larger one for VLP (+15.1 %). We built a three-dimensional FDTD model of the full device (random Ag island film with the AFM statistics on the fitted nanoparticle DBR on glass, light incident from the glass side) and tested about 20 candidate modifications in about 250 simulations.

  1. The particles themselves are optically negligible. One 100 nm VLP- or PS-like sphere per 0.4 × 0.4 µm² (≈ 5 % area coverage, far above the expected surface density) changes the device reflectance by < 0.5 % and moves the minimum by < 0.1 nm.
  2. Changes of the silver alone do not produce a continuous shift. Thin shells (AgCl, protein), extra damping, dissolution and restructuring of the grains either leave the minimum in place (|Δλ| ≤ 5 nm) or, for strong changes, make a second broad minimum near 780 nm the deepest one (a "jump" of +155 to +160 nm), without the recovery of high reflectance at the old dip wavelength that the measurement shows.
  3. The Ag-on-glass control (Fig. S3c,d) is reproduced by loss / restructuring of silver (−5 to −10 %, measured −5.5 % and −12.1 %), not by shells, which raise the reflectance (+5 to +9 %).
  4. A dielectric layer forming at the Ag / TiO2 interface reproduces the measured pattern: a continuous red shift (+36 to +67 nm for 20–80 nm), the short-wavelength stop-band edge moving with it, and the old dip filling up. Extrapolated, ~55–60 nm of AgCl (n ≈ 2.07) or ~110 nm of a hydrated layer (n ≈ 1.33) would give the full ~90 nm. 40 nm of AgCl corresponds to ≈ 16 nm of the 33 nm silver (molar-volume ratio ≈ 2.5), consistent with the silver loss seen on glass.
Working hypothesis. Chloride from PBS penetrates the voids of the island film (and possibly the porous top TiO2 layer) and converts or lifts the silver at the buried interface. The particles act, if at all, through the chemistry of the buffer, not optically. This is a hypothesis to be tested by experiment (Section 7); it is not yet proven.

1. The measured response to be explained

Two control measurements of the supporting information restrict the possible causes:

  • Bare DBR (Fig. S3a,b): the red edge of the stop band moves by ≤ 1 nm after PBS or VLP. Infiltration of the porous DBR alone cannot explain the shift.
  • Ag on glass (Fig. S3c,d): the reflectance of the silver film drops by 5.5 % (PBS) and 12.1 % (VLP) on average over 450–700 nm. The silver itself is modified.

2. Model

2.1 DBR

Transfer-matrix fit of the measured DBR spectrum (Fig. 1 of the paper; R and T, unpolarised, 8° incidence, incoherent 1 mm glass): 5 × (SiO2 / TiO2) nanoparticle layers, SiO2 first on the glass, Bruggeman porosities 4.8 % (SiO2) and 18.2 % (TiO2), thicknesses 100.6 / 75.2 nm (SEM prior 110 / 90 ± 8 nm), thickness spread 5.3 %; rms deviation 2.8 %. In the 3D device the DBR is represented by its effective indices (1.435 / 1.93) and the fitted thicknesses (×0.97, from the device fit).

2.2 Why a 3D model

A planar model with an effective-medium silver layer cannot reproduce the measured device spectrum (best rms 10.6 %, with damping and depolarisation at their bounds). The film is a percolated island film (AFM: grains 50 ± 18 nm, 14.4 % voids, 33 nm nominal thickness), and its near field and scattering into the DBR matter. We checked this directly: cascading the zeroth-order S-matrix of the film with the DBR deviates from the full 3D device by up to 0.33 in R. All device results below are therefore full 3D runs of the complete stack.

2.3 Silver and island film

  • Permittivity: Ag film data of Ciesielski et al. (2017, 20 nm film), fitted with Drude + 2 Lorentz terms for the FDTD (max. relative error 0.18 %, 350–1000 nm).
  • Geometry: random sequential deposition of columnar grains (cylinder + oblate cap), diameters N(50, 18) nm, heights N(1, 0.12) × h, filling until 14.4 % void; heights scaled to a mean thickness of 33 nm. Deterministic for a given seed; 358 grains in the 0.8 µm cell.
  • The model surface roughness (≈ 14 nm rms) is larger than the AFM value (8.7 nm); tip convolution reduces the AFM value in narrow voids, so we kept the measured coverage and thickness as the constraints.

2.4 FDTD set-up (Meep)

  • Periodic P × P cell, normal incidence from the glass side, x polarisation (y checked: dip within 3 nm), Gaussian pulse 380–950 nm, 58 frequencies, PML along z.
  • Grid 5 nm; R and T from Poynting fluxes normalised to identical homogeneous runs; the thick glass is added incoherently (back face).
  • Cell size: P = 0.3 µm cells act as gratings (lattice resonances, strong realisation scatter), so all main results use P = 0.8 µm and the mean of 2–3 random realisations. The measured spot averages many realisations, so the realisation-mean spectrum is compared with the experiment.
  • About 250 runs on GitHub Actions (up to 20 in parallel, up to 6 h each); code and all raw results are in the project repository.

5. Interlayer at the Ag / TiO2 interface

Because no change of the silver alone gave a continuous shift, we scanned single changes in a planar model. A dielectric overlayer on the silver, water as ambient, or a lower plasma frequency all gave |Δλ| ≤ 10 nm. Only a layer between the DBR and the silver, which adds optical path inside the Tamm cavity, moved the dip continuously. We then simulated this in full 3D (round 7, P = 0.8 µm, 2 realisations).

6. Interpretation and limitations

  • Qualitative agreement, not yet quantitative. The interlayer reproduces the type of change (continuous shift, moving stop-band edge, small depth change for a thin AgCl layer and a larger one for a thicker, lower-index layer). However, for a given shift it fills the old dip more than measured (+55 to +80 % against +39 %). A laterally inhomogeneous interlayer, combined silver loss, or a graded interface would broaden and weaken the response; these combinations have not been simulated.
  • Thickness needed. The full ~90 nm needs, by extrapolation, ~55–60 nm of AgCl or ~110 nm of a hydrated layer. This is large compared with a 33 nm film. Partial conversion and lift-off together may be required.
  • Grid sensitivity of the island film (Section 3) affects absolute spectra. The round-8 check tests the robustness of the interlayer result.
  • Normal incidence and one polarisation were simulated (unpolarised, 8° in the experiment). Oblique-incidence corrections at 8° are small for these layers.

7. Suggested experiments and questions

  1. Cross-section (FIB-SEM / TEM) and EDX or XPS depth profile of an exposed device: is there Cl or a gap at the Ag / TiO2 interface?
  2. Chloride-free buffer control (e.g. phosphate buffer without NaCl / KCl): if the shift disappears, chloride is the cause.
  3. Reflectance from the film side and transmission of the same exposed device: these give an independent constraint on where the change sits.
  4. Time series of the dip during exposure: does it move continuously (interlayer growth) or switch?

Questions on the measurement that affect the modelling:

  • Were the "after" spectra recorded dry, after rinsing with water, or wet?
  • Exposure time and temperature; were the bare-DBR and Ag-on-glass controls exposed identically and at the same time?
  • Is there an adhesion layer between the TiO2 and the silver?
